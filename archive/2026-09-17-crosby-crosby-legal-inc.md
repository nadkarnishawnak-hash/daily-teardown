## The setup

Crosby is an actual registered law firm, not legal software, with 50+ licensed attorneys, malpractice insurance, and AI agents doing the first pass on commercial contracts: NDAs, MSAs, DPAs. Fast-growing software companies including Cursor, Clay and Gumloop send it the paper that sits between a signed deal and recognized revenue. Over 90% of documents come back in a few hours instead of weeks.

## The model

- **Economic buyer:** Usually the go-to-market side, sales leadership and revenue ops, not the general counsel. Crosby says outright it was built by lawyers for sales teams to speed up time to signature (crosby.ai, 2026). A contract stuck in review is booked revenue that hasn't landed, which means the budget comes out of a cost-of-delay line, not a legal line. Heads of legal buy too, but as capacity relief rather than headcount.
- **Emotional driver:** The deal is sitting there. Not fear of a bad clause, fear of the quarter slipping while a firm takes six weeks to redline an NDA. Fixed price plus a stated turnaround removes both the legal anxiety and the invoice anxiety in one purchase.
- **Recurring vs one-time:** Priced one-time per document, but behaves like a subscription because contract flow is continuous. A company doing dozens of NDAs, MSAs and DPAs a month becomes a de facto recurring account. The 30% monthly growth in GTM teams compounds on top of existing accounts (Crosby Journal, 2025).
- **Margins:** Not disclosed, but the structure is clear. Cost per document is attorney minutes plus inference; revenue per document is fixed. AI handles the first pass, the attorney reviews and takes liability, so every minute shaved drops to contribution margin. Unlike a traditional firm, getting faster does not shrink the invoice. The offsetting cost is real: 50+ barred attorneys and malpractice insurance are fixed overhead, so utilization is everything.
- **Capital to start:** Crosby raised $80M across two rounds because it is building agents, a client platform and a lateral-hire attorney bench (Crosby Journal, 2025 and 2026). A one-person version of the same archetype needs $2K-$15K: an LLC, a landing page with a published price and turnaround, off-the-shelf AI, and a licensed professional on contract for whatever requires a license. The capital buys credibility and coverage, not tooling.
- **Moat:** Three layers. One, it is a law firm that carries liability and malpractice insurance, which a software vendor legally cannot do. Two, negotiation history: it claims data from negotiating against 76% of the Fortune 100, so it knows which redlines get accepted and which stall a deal (crosby.ai, 2026). Three, brand reference accounts like Cursor and Clay. Layer one is the real barrier, because pure software competitors have to tell customers to get a lawyer to check the output.
- **Distribution:** Founder-led sales into the fast-growing AI and software cluster, then referral inside that network. Public case studies with recognizable logos do the selling. Cooley LLP and Patrick Collison investing in the Series A is distribution disguised as capital (Crosby Journal, 2025).

## Why it works

The billable hour taxes competence: if you double your speed you halve your invoice, so no law firm has a financial reason to industrialize. Crosby breaks that by fixing the price to the document instead of the clock, which turns every minute of AI leverage into margin rather than a discount. Because the scope is deliberately narrow (NDAs, MSAs, DPAs), the same document shapes repeat thousands of times, so each review makes the next one faster and more predictable, which is what lets them promise hours instead of weeks. That promise is what the buyer is actually paying for, and because the buyer is the revenue team rather than legal, the comparison is not a cheaper lawyer, it is a faster close. Then the firm structure, barred attorneys and malpractice insurance, lets the client fully offload the work instead of babysitting a tool, which is why volume scaled from 1,000 contracts in 173 days to 1,000 every three weeks.

## Where else this shows up

- **Superside**: Sells design and creative work as flat-rate subscription tiers with defined scope and turnaround instead of an agency estimate, so speed gains become margin, not a smaller bill.
- **Pilot**: Startup bookkeeping and tax sold as a fixed monthly price by company stage, with a standardized close process behind it, replacing the hourly accountant relationship.
- **Design Pickle**: The original template: one flat monthly fee, an explicitly bounded request list, unlimited requests processed in a queue, delivery run as an assembly line.

## How you'd start one today

- **First step:** Pick the single most repetitive deliverable in a field you know, then publish the two things incumbents refuse to publish: one price and one turnaround time. This week, write a one-page offer with exact inclusions and exclusions, take it to five people who currently buy that work hourly, and ask what turnaround would make them switch. Deliver the first twenty by hand and document every step, because the process notes are the product.
- **Hardest part:** Variance, and then liability. On a fixed fee, one messy job eats the profit from three clean ones, so copycats die from scope creep they were too polite to refuse. And the moment you sell an outcome rather than hours, you own the downside: Crosby carries full malpractice insurance and takes liability for its work, which is exactly the expensive, unglamorous part imitators skip.

## Takeaway

If you bill by the hour, getting better at your job lowers your income. Fix the price to a tightly defined outcome and every efficiency you find becomes margin instead of a smaller invoice, which is the only way a service business gets software-like economics.
